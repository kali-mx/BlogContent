Running a Honeypot: what internet scanners actually try

Stand up a box that looks like a juicy, misconfigured target, point it at the open internet, and watch what shows up. The results are less "sophisticated APT" and more automated scanners running the same handful of exploit checks on repeat.

Category: Honeypot / Threat Intel Tools: Shodan, nmap, Metasploit

The setup: a deliberately exposed host advertising a wide, deliberately vulnerable-looking service footprint (SMBv1, old Apache Tomcat, exposed databases, an Elasticsearch honeypot, and more), left running to see what automated internet-wide scanning actually looks like in practice.

The Investigation

1
Confirm what the box is already advertising

A Shodan lookup on the honeypot's own IP shows what the rest of the internet already sees before any traffic analysis even starts: close to 400 open ports, flagged with known CVEs like the SMBv1 remote code execution bug and the Exim mail server vulnerability, across a huge mix of services, SSH, mail, RDP, databases, VNC, and an industrial SCADA protocol among them.

Why this works

Shodan indexes the whole internet continuously, so checking your own exposure through it shows exactly what an opportunistic attacker's reconnaissance would show, no active scanning required on your part.

2
Watch automated exploit probes arrive

Traffic logs on the honeypot's web-facing services show automated tooling testing for specific, well-known vulnerabilities, not manual exploration. A probe against the Apache Tomcat AJP connector tests for the Ghostcat file-read vulnerability (CVE-2020-1938):

python2 ghost.py <target>
Getting resource at ajp13://<target>:8009/asdf

A separate Metasploit module checks the SMB service for EternalBlue (MS17-010), and an FTP brute-force script works through a list of common default credentials, several of which succeed against the deliberately weak honeypot logins.

Why this works

None of this traffic is targeted, it's the same small set of high-value, well-known CVE checks that automated scanners run against every IP they encounter. A honeypot makes that background noise visible instead of invisible.

3
Confirm the deliberately weak services actually get found

The honeypot's SMB service reports authentication disabled and SMBv1 enabled, with shares openly listed. Its fake Elasticsearch honeypot, ElasticHoney, a tool specifically designed to catch Elasticsearch RCE attempts, shows the same pattern: automated scanners test it exactly as they would a real misconfigured instance.

Why this works

A deliberately weak service doesn't need to wait long to get probed. Within the observation window here, every exposed, misconfigured-looking service attracted automated attention, confirming that default-credential and legacy-protocol exposure really is scanned for continuously, not theoretically.

Takeaways

Internet-facing exposure gets found fast

Every deliberately weak service on this honeypot was probed by automated tooling, not eventually, but as part of routine, continuous internet-wide scanning. There's no "too small to be a target."

Scanners check specific, well-known CVEs, not zero-days

The actual observed traffic was Ghostcat, EternalBlue, and default-credential checks, all old, well-documented vulnerabilities. Patching known issues promptly defeats the overwhelming majority of opportunistic scanning.

A honeypot is a cheap, direct threat-intel source

Rather than reading about what attackers are doing, a honeypot shows it directly: the exact services, exact exploit checks, and exact credential lists currently in active use against exposed infrastructure.
